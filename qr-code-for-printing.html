What “print-ready” actually means

A print-ready QR code has the correct destination, enough physical size for its scan distance, dark modules on a light background, and an uninterrupted quiet zone. Its artwork is sharp at final size and it has been tested from the finished material, not only from a screen.

Static vs dynamic before you print anything

A static QR code stores the destination directly and cannot be changed after printing. It is a dependable choice for permanent URLs and fixed details. A dynamic service redirects scans through another URL, which may let you change the destination later but adds a provider dependency. For a static code that may need a new destination, encode a short URL on a domain you control and manage its redirect yourself.

File format: SVG first, PNG only at final size

Export SVG for print; use PNG only if the printer demands a raster file at 300–600 DPI at final size. SVG artwork can be resized without softening the square edges. Do not enlarge a small PNG inside design software and assume its stated DPI will restore missing detail.

  • Ask the printer whether its workflow accepts SVG and whether fonts or transparency need special handling.
  • Keep the QR code square; never stretch it to fit a box.
  • If supplying PNG, calculate the pixel dimensions from the final physical size and requested DPI.

Minimum size and the 10:1 scan-distance rule

As a planning baseline, make the QR code about one tenth of the expected scan distance. A code scanned from 30 cm starts at roughly 3 cm wide. A simple URL code used at arm’s length should usually be no smaller than 2 × 2 cm, including its quiet zone. Dense data, logos, weak lighting and unusual surfaces require more space.

Error correction, contrast and quiet zone

Use a dark foreground on a plain light background. Preserve a quiet zone at least four modules wide on every side; this border is part of the code, not spare design space. Medium error correction is suitable for many clean codes. Higher error correction can tolerate limited damage or a small central logo, but it also makes the pattern denser, so enlarge and retest the code.

Paper, lamination, windows and packaging

  • Paper: uncoated or matte stock usually produces less glare than gloss.
  • Lamination: use a matte finish and test beneath the venue’s actual lights.
  • Windows: allow for reflections, tint, rain and scanning from the intended side.
  • Packaging: keep the code on a flat area, away from seams, folds and strong curves.

Test print checklist

  1. Confirm the encoded destination and all tracking parameters.
  2. Print one copy at final size on the intended stock or finish.
  3. Scan with at least one iPhone and one Android phone.
  4. Test at the real distance, angle and lowest likely light level.
  5. Check that the destination loads on mobile data, not only office WiFi.
  6. Inspect the final proof after trimming, folding or lamination.
  7. Make sure nearby copy tells people what they will get by scanning.
